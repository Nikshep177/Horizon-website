# To those we would do well to remember

Science is objective, and methodical. Logic and reasoning go hand in hand with Physics. However, it is us humans who practice these disciplines. And where humans go, biases creep in. This article is all about giving recognition where it is due, acknowledging those minds whose great contributions have been instrumental towards our collective progress. Let us hear all about a bunch of cool people!

Vera Rubin (1928–2016) was an American astronomer whose groundbreaking observations transformed cosmology. Despite gender discrimination that barred her from full observatory access and prestigious prizes like the Nobel, she became a trailblazer, honoured posthumously with the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile. Rubin measured rotation speeds of stars and gas in spiral galaxies like Andromeda using a custom image-tube spectrograph with Kent Ford. She found flat rotation curves—stars at the edges orbiting at nearly the same speed as those near the center, defying Newtonian gravity. This "galaxy rotation problem," observed across over 60 galaxies by the 1980s, showed visible matter alone couldn't account for the dynamics. Her data provided direct evidence for dark matter, an invisible mass comprising about 85% of a galaxy's total—up to 90% in some cases—holding galaxies together via unseen gravity. This Copernican-scale shift, building on Fritz Zwicky's earlier hints, confirmed dark matter's role in galaxy formation and cosmic structure. Her work reshaped theories from the Big Bang to galaxy clusters, yet no Nobel came—speculation persists it eluded her due to biases. Today, her legacy drives dark matter hunts via gravitational lensing and missions like NASA's Roman Space Telescope

Bibha Chowdhuri (1913–1991) was a pioneering Indian physicist and one of the earliest women in particle physics, renowned for her cosmic ray research during a time when such fields were deemed "unsuitable for women" in pre-independence India. Overcoming rejection from her mentor Debendra Mohan Bose at the Bose Institute, she joined him in grueling high-altitude expeditions—traveling by mule into the Himalayas to stations in Darjeeling, Sandakphu, and Tibet. In the late 1930s and 1940s, Chowdhuri and Bose exposed Ilford half-tone photographic emulsion plates to cosmic rays at altitudes up to 14,500 feet for 150–200 days, analyzing particle tracks left behind. Their work revealed tracks from lighter particles than protons—matching Hideki Yukawa's predicted mesons (later identified as muons or mesotrons)—with curved decays due to multiple scattering and reduced mass at lower altitudes. Published in Nature (1940–1941), these findings provided early experimental validation of Yukawa's theory, though WWII embargoes on sensitive plates delayed precision until Cecil Powell's later Nobel-winning work acknowledged them. Earning her PhD from the University of Manchester in 1950 on extensive air showers, Chowdhuri became TIFR's first female faculty member under Homi Bhabha, contributing to K-meson discoveries and scintillator detectors. She later advanced air shower arrays at MIT's New Mexico lab and India's Kolar Gold Fields with Vikram Sarabhai, refining models of secondary particles and cosmic ray showers. Gender biases barred her from full credit—Bose's name often overshadowed hers, and colonial-era resource limits marginalized Indian scientists. Despite newspaper acclaim as "India's New Woman" with "an eye for cosmic rays," she received no major awards in her lifetime, her legacy resurfacing only posthumously through books and tributes. Chowdhuri's tenacity advanced particle physics, proving cosmic rays as natural particle accelerators when man-made ones were unavailable. 

Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) is often remembered as a luminous star of Hollywood’s Golden Age, but behind the screen persona was a technically gifted inventor whose ideas anticipated modern secure wireless communication. Disturbed by reports of Nazi attacks and drawing on her experience with military hardware from a previous marriage to an Austrian arms dealer, she began working on the problem of radio guided torpedoes being easily jammed. In collaboration with composer George Antheil, Lamarr designed a “secret communication system” in which a transmitter and receiver would hop together through a sequence of radio frequencies, making it almost impossible for an enemy to lock on and disrupt the signal. They encoded the hopping pattern using a mechanism inspired by player piano rolls, and in 1942 were granted a U.S. patent for what we now recognize as an early form of spread spectrum, frequency hopping technology. The U.S. Navy, steeped in sexism and scepticism toward civilian ideas, initially dismissed the invention and instead suggested Lamarr help the war effort by selling bonds, reinforcing stereotypes that a glamorous actress could not also be a serious technologist. Only decades later did the military quietly adopt frequency hopping concepts for secure communications and anti submarine warfare, and the broader telecommunications industry eventually used spread spectrum as a foundation for GPS, Wi Fi, Bluetooth, and cellular networks. Lamarr received little credit or financial reward during her lifetime; recognition came late, in the 1990s, when she was honoured by the Electronic Frontier Foundation and others as a pioneer of wireless security. Her story exposes how gendered expectations can bury transformative technical work, and why the history of communication technology must be rewritten to include her as a genuine co architect of the wireless world.

Meghnad Saha (1893–1956) was an Indian astrophysicist whose work quietly underpins how we read the light of stars. Born into a poor family in rural Bengal, he fought his way through the colonial education system, eventually studying in Calcutta and later working alongside contemporaries like S.N. Bose and C.V. Raman. In the early 1920s, Saha tackled a central puzzle in astrophysics: why stellar spectra display certain absorption lines, and how these relate to temperature and ionization in stellar atmospheres. His answer, now known as the Saha ionization equation, linked the ionization state of a gas to its temperature, pressure, and electron density, allowing astronomers to translate spectral lines into physical conditions. This turned stellar spectroscopy from a qualitative catalog into a quantitative diagnostic, essential to the later development of stellar classification and evolution theory. Saha’s equation became foundational for astrophysics, yet his location in colonial India limited his visibility in European and American scientific networks. Despite being elected to several learned societies and having the Saha Institute of Nuclear Physics named in his honor, he never received the global recognition or prizes that might have gone to a comparable scientist working in the imperial centers. Beyond theory, Saha was deeply engaged with nation building: he helped design the Damodar Valley Project, advocated for scientific planning in independent India, and even served in the first Lok Sabha to shape policy on education, industry, and river management. His life illustrates how world class science can emerge far from traditional hubs, yet still be filtered by colonial hierarchies in the historical record. For modern astrophysics students, every time spectral lines are used to infer stellar temperatures, Saha’s quiet revolution is at work in the background.

Lise Meitner (1878–1968) was an Austrian born physicist whose lucid thinking transformed puzzling nuclear data into the concept of nuclear fission, even as antisemitism and sexism kept her name off the Nobel citation. One of the first women admitted to the University of Vienna, she later moved to Berlin, where she began a decades long collaboration with chemist Otto Hahn, studying radioactivity and neutron induced reactions. As Nazi racial laws tightened, Meitner, a Jewish scientist, was forced to flee Germany in 1938, eventually securing a precarious post in Stockholm. From exile she continued to correspond with Hahn, who, with Fritz Strassmann, had bombarded uranium with neutrons and found barium among the products—an apparently impossible result under existing nuclear models. During a now legendary 1938 walk in the Swedish snow with her nephew Otto Frisch, Meitner re interpreted Hahn’s data: the uranium nucleus must be splitting into two lighter nuclei, releasing immense energy as predicted by Einstein’s $$E = mc^2$$. She and Frisch coined the term “fission” and published the first theoretical explanation in early 1939, laying the conceptual groundwork for both nuclear reactors and weapons. Yet when the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded, it went solely to Hahn for the discovery of fission; Meitner’s essential theoretical leap, and her role in the decades of prior joint work, were overlooked, a decision historians now widely interpret as shaped by gender bias and wartime politics. Meitner herself refused to participate in the Manhattan Project, insisting she would “have nothing to do with a bomb,” maintaining a moral stance that her epitaph captures: “Lise Meitner: a physicist who never lost her humanity.” Her story is a stark example of how scientific credit can be severed from scientific contribution.

Eunice Newton Foote (1819–1888) was an American scientist, inventor, and women’s rights advocate whose simple yet incisive experiments anticipated the physics of the greenhouse effect. Working outside formal academic institutions in the mid 19th century, she set up glass cylinders filled with different gases—ordinary air, moist air, and air enriched with “carbonic acid gas” (carbon dioxide)—and exposed them to sunlight while monitoring temperature changes with thermometers. She observed that the CO₂ rich cylinder warmed more and retained heat longer than the others, leading her to conclude that “an atmosphere of that gas would give to our earth a high temperature.” This 1856 insight directly linked atmospheric composition to planetary warming, essentially sketching the core idea of anthropogenic climate change long before the industrial scale emissions of the 20th century. Foote’s paper was read at the American Association for the Advancement of Science meeting in Albany—but not by her; a male colleague presented it, reflecting the era’s gender norms in scientific forums. Her work appeared in brief conference proceedings and popular science magazines but did not propagate into the mainstream physics literature. A few years later, John Tyndall performed more elaborate experiments on the absorption of infrared radiation by gases and became widely credited as the discoverer of the greenhouse effect, while Foote’s contribution slipped into obscurity. Only in the 21st century have historians of science rediscovered her paper and recognized her as the first to experimentally demonstrate CO₂ driven warming. That rediscovery not only corrects the record but also underscores how women’s experimental work, especially outside elite institutions, could be quietly erased from canonical narratives, even when it anticipated some of the defining scientific questions of our time.

Satyendra Nath Bose (1894–1974) was an Indian theoretical physicist whose elegant rethinking of how particles occupy quantum states reshaped statistical mechanics and opened the path to Bose–Einstein condensation. In 1924, while teaching in Dacca, he revisited Planck’s derivation of blackbody radiation and noticed that treating photons as distinguishable particles was conceptually inconsistent. Instead, he proposed counting their states in a new way, assuming indistinguishability and allowing multiple particles to share a single quantum state. This led to a derivation of Planck’s law without classical assumptions and introduced what we now call Bose–Einstein statistics, first for photons and later generalized by Einstein to atoms. Bose initially struggled to publish his paper in European journals; frustrated, he sent it directly to Albert Einstein, who immediately grasped its importance, translated it into German, and arranged its publication. Einstein then extended Bose’s method to massive particles, predicting that at very low temperatures they would “condense” into a single quantum state—the Bose–Einstein condensate realized experimentally only in 1995, earning a later generation the Nobel Prize. Today, all particles with integer spin are called “bosons” in his honor, and Bose–Einstein statistics underlie phenomena from superfluidity to cutting edge quantum technologies. Yet, working from colonial India with limited access to the main European centers, Bose did not build the large research school his ideas deserved, and his name is often encountered by students only as a label in textbooks rather than as part of a vivid personal story. His career exemplifies how deep theoretical innovation can emerge from the periphery of empire, and how structural inequities can mute the recognition accorded to such innovation.

Alan Turing (1912–1954) was an English mathematician, logician, and cryptanalyst whose ideas laid the conceptual foundation for modern computing while his wartime codebreaking altered the course of World War II. In 1936, he introduced the abstract “Turing machine,” a simple symbolic device capable of performing any computation that can be algorithmically specified, thereby formalizing what it means for a function to be computable. This work not only resolved deep questions in mathematical logic but also provided the blueprint for general purpose digital computers. With the outbreak of war, Turing joined Britain’s Government Code and Cypher School at Bletchley Park, where he led efforts to attack the German Enigma cipher. Turing designed the electromechanical Bombe, a machine that systematically searched through the astronomical number of Enigma settings by exploiting probable plaintext fragments (“cribs”) and implementing chains of logical deductions in hardware. These machines, refined with colleagues like Gordon Welchman, enabled decryption of vast volumes of German military traffic, providing intelligence (ULTRA) that historians estimate shortened the war by years and saved countless lives. After the war, Turing worked on early stored program computers and even anticipated ideas in artificial intelligence, proposing the famous “Turing test” for machine intelligence. Yet his immense contributions remained largely classified, and instead of being celebrated, he was prosecuted in 1952 for homosexual acts, then criminal in Britain. Forced to undergo chemical castration and barred from sensitive work, he died in 1954 under circumstances widely believed to be suicide. Only decades later did the British government issue an apology and a royal pardon, and did the public narrative begin to acknowledge Turing as one of the central figures in the creation of the digital age whose life was tragically warped by prejudice.
