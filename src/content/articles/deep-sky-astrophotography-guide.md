Deep-sky astrophotography lets you capture objects beyond our solar system — nebulae, galaxies, and star clusters. This guide covers the essentials for getting started with minimal equipment.

## What You'll Need

### Camera

You don't need an expensive dedicated astronomy camera to start:

- **DSLR / Mirrorless**: A modern camera with manual controls works well
- **Tracking mount**: A star tracker (like Sky-Watcher Star Adventurer or iOptron SkyGuider) lets you take longer exposures without star trails
- **Tripod**: A sturdy tripod is essential

### Software

- **Stacking**: DeepSkyStacker (free, Windows) or Siril (free, cross-platform)
- **Processing**: Photoshop, GIMP, or PixInsight
- **Acquisition**: Many cameras work with digiCamControl or the native intervalometer

## Step 1: Capture Your Frames

### Lights (The actual data)

Take multiple exposures of your target:

```
ISO: 800–1600
Aperture: Wide open (f/2.8 or f/4)
Shutter: 30–120 seconds (depends on tracker)
Frames: 50–200
```

### Calibration Frames

These remove sensor noise and optical artifacts:

- **Darks**: Same settings as lights but with lens cap on
- **Flats**: Uniformly illuminated frames to fix vignetting and dust
- **Bias**: Shortest possible exposure at same ISO

## Step 2: Stack

Load your frames into DeepSkyStacker or Siril. The software aligns and combines them to produce a single, high-signal image with reduced noise.

## Step 3: Process

Post-processing brings out the detail:

1. **Stretch the histogram** — most of the data is in the dark end
2. **Adjust levels and curves**
3. **Reduce background gradients**
4. **Enhance colour and contrast**
5. **Sharpen cautiously**
